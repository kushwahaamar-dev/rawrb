\begin{abstract}
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting improves reasoning in small language models, but do generated explanations \emph{causally determine} the answer? We introduce \textbf{RAWRB}, a diagnostic framework with four causal probes---knockout, corruption, counterfactual, and paraphrase stability---plus a question-only control and prompt sensitivity ablation, testing whether CoT steps are faithful or post-hoc rationalizations. Evaluating five open-source models across two size tiers (1.5--3.8B and 7--8B) on GSM8K, MATH, and FOLIO, we observe a \emph{faithfulness--capability tradeoff}: within this range, medium-tier models achieve ${\sim}$20pp higher accuracy but 11--18pp \emph{lower} faithfulness on perturbation probes, suggesting that improved accuracy does not entail improved faithfulness. We further demonstrate a strong CoT anchoring effect---stale CoT suppresses answer-change rates by 50--70pp---and show that counterfactual faithfulness scores are inflated 42--67pp by consistency-checking instructions, highlighting prompt sensitivity as a key confound. All experiments use locally-run models via Ollama for full reproducibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

CoT prompting~\cite{wei2022chain} enables small language models (SLMs) to solve multi-step reasoning tasks. A natural assumption is that CoT steps \emph{explain} the model's answer---that the reasoning chain causally determines the output. Recent work shows this assumption fails for large proprietary models~\cite{turpin2024language,lanham2023measuring}: models produce correct answers alongside unfaithful reasoning. As SLMs ($\leq$9B parameters) are increasingly deployed in edge, mobile, and cost-sensitive settings, understanding whether their explanations can be trusted is critical for safety-critical applications and user trust in model-generated reasoning.

We ask: \emph{does this hold for the open-source SLMs increasingly deployed in practice?} And critically: \emph{does faithfulness improve with capability?}

We introduce \textbf{RAWRB} (Right Answer, Wrong Reason Benchmark), a diagnostic framework with four causal probes testing whether CoT steps causally determine answers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Knockout}: removing a critical step should change the answer
    \item \textbf{Corruption}: injecting an error should propagate through the CoT
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual}: mismatched premise + stale CoT should be detected
    \item \textbf{Paraphrase}: equivalent questions should yield structurally consistent CoTs
\end{enumerate}
Our knockout and corruption probes follow the perturbation paradigm of Lanham et al.~\cite{lanham2023measuring}; our primary contributions are extending this to open-source SLMs with (a)~a \textbf{question-only control} and (b)~an \textbf{unbiased prompt ablation} for the counterfactual probe, enabling causal interpretation absent from prior work.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions:} (1)~A causal probe suite for CoT faithfulness in open-source SLMs with novel control conditions; (2)~a two-tier analysis (1.5--3.8B vs.\ 7--8B) revealing a faithfulness--capability tradeoff with per-benchmark and within-family evidence; (3)~discovery of a CoT anchoring effect and quantification of prompt sensitivity as a confound; (4)~a contamination-sensitivity analysis across benchmarks of varying contamination risk; (5)~full reproducibility via locally-run Ollama models with code release.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: \S\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, \S\ref{sec:method} describes our methodology, \S\ref{sec:results} presents results across seven analytical dimensions, \S\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications, and \S\ref{sec:limitations} addresses limitations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{CoT Faithfulness.} Jacovi and Goldberg~\cite{jacovi2020towards} distinguish plausibility from faithfulness: a \emph{faithful} explanation causally determines the output. Turpin et al.~\cite{turpin2024language} showed CoT can be biased by irrelevant context in large models. Lanham et al.~\cite{lanham2023measuring} developed perturbation probes---early answering, filler tokens, and content corruption---for large proprietary models; our knockout and corruption probes adapt this paradigm to the SLM regime, adding control conditions they did not include. Ye and Durrett~\cite{ye2022unreliability} found free-text rationales frequently unfaithful. Lyu et al.~\cite{lyu2023faithful} proposed symbolic translation for faithful CoT. Radhakrishnan et al.~\cite{radhakrishnan2023question} used question decomposition to improve faithfulness. Parcalabescu and Frank~\cite{parcalabescu2024measuring} proposed intervention-based measures complementary to ours. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023understanding} examined the role of text patterns in CoT effectiveness, finding that even invalid reasoning chains can improve performance---consistent with our finding that CoT may serve as a retrieval cue rather than a causal reasoning trace.

\paragraph{Prompt Sensitivity.} Recent work has shown that LLM evaluation is highly sensitive to prompt design~\cite{sclar2024quantifying}. Minor changes in instruction phrasing can shift performance by 10--70pp. Our unbiased counterfactual ablation directly tests this concern in the faithfulness evaluation context, finding 42--67pp inflation from a single consistency-checking instruction.

\paragraph{Benchmark Contamination.} GSM8K and MATH are widely used in pre-training corpora, raising contamination concerns~\cite{golchin2024time}. A model that has memorized answers may appear unfaithful on perturbation probes (ignoring CoT manipulations to produce memorized answers). We include FOLIO---a less common first-order logic benchmark---as a partial control, and present a contamination-sensitivity analysis comparing faithfulness across benchmarks of varying contamination risk (\S\ref{sec:contamination}).

\paragraph{SLM Reasoning.} CoT~\cite{wei2022chain}, self-consistency~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, and program-aided reasoning~\cite{chen2023program,gao2023pal} have improved SLM performance. Knowledge distillation~\cite{wang2024survey} further closes the gap with larger models. We complement these efforts by asking: when SLMs reason correctly, do they reason faithfully?

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{CoT-Obedience as Faithfulness}

Following~\cite{jacovi2020towards}, we define faithfulness as \emph{causal responsibility}: a CoT is faithful iff it causally determines the output. Our probes operationalize this as \textbf{CoT-obedience}---whether the model's answer is determined by the reasoning chain it was given.

Importantly, faithfulness $\neq$ correctness. A model that ignores a corrupted CoT to produce the correct answer is \emph{capable} but \emph{unfaithful}: its output is not causally determined by its stated reasoning. Conversely, a model that follows corrupted reasoning to a wrong answer is \emph{faithful} but \emph{incorrect}.

This operationalization admits three outcomes for perturbation probes: (1)~\textbf{faithful engagement}---the perturbation changes the answer because the model genuinely follows the CoT; (2)~\textbf{unfaithful bypassing}---the answer is unchanged because the model ignores the CoT; (3)~\textbf{competent reconstruction}---the answer is unchanged because the model infers the missing/corrupted information from context. Our probes cannot fully distinguish (2) from (3); we address this via stratified analysis (\S\ref{sec:stratified}).

\subsection{Benchmarks}

We evaluate on three benchmarks spanning different reasoning domains and contamination risk levels: \textbf{GSM8K}~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k} (200 sampled, multi-step arithmetic; \emph{high} contamination risk), \textbf{MATH}~\cite{hendrycks2021math} (200 sampled across 7 subjects, competition math; \emph{moderate} contamination risk), and \textbf{FOLIO}~\cite{han2022folio} (200 sampled from the 203-item validation set, first-order logic; \emph{low} contamination risk). This three-benchmark design enables contamination-sensitivity analysis (\S\ref{sec:contamination}). MATH problems include difficulty levels (1--5), enabling per-difficulty analysis. All sampling uses \texttt{random.Random(42)}.

\subsection{Probes}

\paragraph{Knockout.} Remove one computationally critical CoT step; ask the model to continue. Faithful $\Rightarrow$ answer changes. Steps containing numbers or logical connectives are preferentially targeted.

\paragraph{Corruption.} Inject a numeric error (${\geq}30\%$ relative change) into a CoT step with \emph{neutral} framing: ``Use the provided reasoning to determine the final answer.'' No instruction to accept or reject the reasoning. Faithful $\Rightarrow$ error propagates.

\paragraph{Counterfactual.} Modify a key question value; keep original CoT. The prompt includes a consistency-checking instruction: ``First check whether the analysis is consistent with the problem. If it is not, adjust your reasoning accordingly.'' Faithful $\Rightarrow$ answer changes.

\paragraph{Question-Only Control.} Same modified question, \emph{no CoT provided}. Comparison with counterfactual reveals whether answer changes stem from CoT engagement or question comprehension alone.

\paragraph{Counterfactual (Unbiased).} Identical to the counterfactual probe but \emph{without} the consistency-checking instruction. The prompt simply says: ``Use the analysis to determine the final answer.'' Comparing the two variants isolates prompt sensitivity.

\paragraph{Paraphrase Stability.} 3 rephrasings per question ($T{=}0.7$); CoT consistency via weighted Jaccard keyword similarity (0.7) + step-count similarity (0.3).

\subsection{Metrics and Statistics}

\textbf{Per-probe faithfulness}: proportion of appropriate responses (answer changed for knockout/corruption/counterfactual; consistent CoT for paraphrase). All scores with 95\% bootstrap CIs ($n{=}10{,}000$, seed=7919). Gap significance: bootstrap gap test ($n{=}10{,}000$ resamples under $H_0$: gap $= 0$). Counterfactual vs.\ question-only: paired McNemar's test with continuity correction. Prompt sensitivity (CF vs.\ CF\textsubscript{U}): paired McNemar's test. Multiple comparisons: Benjamini-Hochberg correction at FDR=0.05. Effect sizes: Cohen's $h$. Faithfulness stratified by baseline correctness. JSON parse error rows are excluded; effective $n$ ranges from 116--399 per cell (\S\ref{sec:errors}).

\subsection{Faithfulness by Probe}

\autoref{tab:faithfulness} presents aggregate faithfulness scores and \autoref{fig:heatmap} shows per-probe faithfulness across models. Knockout and corruption faithfulness are uniformly low (10--41\%), indicating models frequently arrive at answers independently of their stated reasoning.

\textbf{Within the 1.5--8B range, we observe a faithfulness--capability tradeoff}: medium-tier models show \emph{lower} faithfulness on knockout (16.8\% vs.\ 34.4\%) and corruption (15.7\% vs.\ 26.5\%) compared to small-tier models, despite ${\sim}$20pp higher accuracy. This tradeoff is statistically robust: a permutation test ($n{=}10{,}000$) confirms the tier difference is significant ($p < 0.0001$) for both probes, with non-overlapping 95\% bootstrap CIs. The correlation between accuracy and faithfulness is strongly negative (Spearman's $\rho{=}{-}0.90$, $p{=}0.037$ for knockout; $\rho{=}{-}1.00$, $p{<}0.001$ for corruption). The Qwen-2.5 within-family comparison controls for architecture: accuracy increases from 39.3\% to 69.5\% (+30pp), while knockout faithfulness \emph{decreases} from 35.9\% to 9.7\% (-26pp). We note that with only two medium-tier models, this pattern is suggestive rather than definitive, and alternative explanations---including benchmark contamination (\S\ref{sec:contamination}) and stronger pre-training priors---could contribute.

\subsection{The Faithfulness Gap}
\label{sec:stratified}

The faithfulness gap (accuracy $-$ faithfulness) \emph{widens} with capability: from $+12.9$pp (small) to $+50.1$pp (medium) for knockout, and from $+20.8$pp to $+51.2$pp for corruption (all $p < 0.001$ after BH correction; \autoref{fig:gap}). Larger models increasingly arrive at correct answers while ignoring their stated reasoning.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/faithfulness_gap.pdf}
    \caption{Accuracy vs.\ per-probe faithfulness for each model. The gap between blue bars (accuracy) and orange/yellow bars (knockout/corruption faithfulness) widens for more capable models.}
    \label{fig:gap}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Concordance: right answer \emph{and} right reason.} Only 4--14\% of problems are simultaneously correct \emph{and} faithful on knockout---meaning models get the right answer \emph{for the right reason} less than one-seventh of the time. The most capable model (Qwen-2.5-7B) has the lowest concordance (4.4\%).

\paragraph{Stratified by baseline correctness.} \autoref{tab:conditional} reveals a striking asymmetry. Among baseline-\emph{correct} items, knockout faithfulness is only 6--28\%, meaning \textbf{72--94\% of correctly-solved problems have answers invariant to CoT perturbation}---strong evidence that correct answers are retrieved independently of stated reasoning. Among baseline-\emph{incorrect} items, faithfulness is substantially higher (18--54\%), consistent with more fragile reasoning chains that are genuinely engaged during generation. The asymmetry holds for all five models (Delta = -11.6pp to -29.4pp), arguing against the ``competent reconstruction'' alternative: if models were simply inferring removed steps from context, we would expect similar faithfulness rates regardless of baseline correctness.

\subsection{Counterfactual Controls and Prompt Sensitivity}
\label{sec:controls}

Three conditions reveal a CoT \emph{anchoring effect}. Without any CoT, models detect question modifications 68--75\% of the time (QO). Adding stale CoT \emph{without} a consistency hint drops this to 3--17\% (CF\textsubscript{U})---the CoT anchors models to the original answer. Adding the consistency instruction partially overcomes anchoring for most models (CF: 58--80\%) but not for Qwen-2.5-1.5B (CF: 5\%, QO: 71\%), which shows extreme anchoring.

\paragraph{Prompt sensitivity quantification.} \autoref{tab:prompt_sens} presents the full prompt sensitivity analysis. The gap between CF and CF\textsubscript{U} is statistically significant ($p < 0.0001$) for 12 of 15 model$\times$benchmark cells, with Cohen's $h$ ranging from 0.71 to 1.85 (large to enormous effects). The three non-significant cells all belong to Qwen-2.5-1.5B, which has near-zero counterfactual faithfulness regardless of prompt.

This has methodological implications: faithfulness evaluations using leading prompts may substantially overestimate faithfulness. We recommend that future evaluations report both prompted and unprompted variants.

\subsection{Benchmark Effects and Contamination Signals}
\label{sec:contamination}

If the faithfulness--capability tradeoff were purely driven by benchmark contamination, we would expect \emph{uniformly} lower faithfulness on highly contaminated benchmarks (GSM8K) than on less contaminated ones (FOLIO). \autoref{tab:contam} shows the signal is \textbf{mixed}: Qwen-2.5-1.5B and Phi-3 Mini show higher knockout faithfulness on GSM8K than FOLIO (+18.9pp and +25.3pp, consistent with contamination), while Llama-3.2 and Llama-3.1 show the reverse ($-$16.1pp and $-$23.8pp). This mixed pattern suggests contamination contributes to but does not solely explain the observed faithfulness patterns.

\paragraph{Per-difficulty MATH analysis.} Within the MATH benchmark, knockout faithfulness \emph{increases} with problem difficulty: Level~1 (easiest) shows 34\% faithfulness while Level~5 (hardest) shows 42\%. This is consistent with the contamination interpretation: easier problems are more likely memorized, leading models to bypass CoT perturbations and produce stored answers.

\paragraph{Probe cross-validation.} Knockout and corruption probes agree on 68--89\% of problems (Cohen's $\kappa{=}0.29$--$0.39$, fair agreement), confirming they measure a related underlying property rather than independent artifacts.

\paragraph{Within-family Qwen comparison.} The controlled within-family analysis provides the strongest evidence because it eliminates architectural confounds. \autoref{tab:qwen} shows that all six benchmark$\times$probe deltas are negative: scaling from 1.5B to 7B consistently reduces faithfulness, with knockout drops of $-$15.4pp to $-$30.6pp and corruption drops of $-$13.4pp to $-$24.9pp. The absence of any reversal across benchmarks strengthens the tradeoff interpretation.

\autoref{fig:paraphrase} shows the distribution of CoT consistency scores across models. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{A faithfulness--capability tradeoff.} Within the 1.5--8B range, we observe that more capable models show lower perturbation faithfulness, suggesting that scaling improves answer retrieval through mechanisms partly independent of CoT reasoning. The Qwen-2.5 within-family comparison---controlling for architecture---shows a $5\times$ parameter increase yielding $+30$pp accuracy but $-26$pp knockout faithfulness, with all six benchmark$\times$probe deltas negative (\autoref{tab:qwen}). The per-benchmark analysis reveals this effect is concentrated on GSM8K (Cohen's $h{=}{-}0.54$) and less pronounced on FOLIO ($h{=}0.002$), suggesting that contamination is a contributing factor. However, the mixed contamination signal across model families (\autoref{tab:contam}) and the universal within-family pattern argue that contamination alone is insufficient to explain the tradeoff. We emphasize this is observed within a narrow parameter range with $n{=}5$ models, and should not be read as a universal scaling law.

\paragraph{The anchoring effect.} The gap between question-only (68--75\%) and unbiased counterfactual (3--17\%) answer-change rates reveals that CoT exerts a strong anchoring effect: models presented with stale reasoning largely ignore question modifications they would otherwise detect. CoT can thus \emph{undermine} faithfulness by anchoring models to potentially outdated reasoning---a finding with practical implications for retrieval-augmented generation and multi-turn dialogue settings where context evolves. Practitioners should avoid feeding cached CoTs to models across context changes and should design evaluation pipelines that test for anchoring.

\paragraph{Implications for faithfulness evaluation.} The 42--67pp prompt sensitivity in counterfactual probes (Cohen's $h$ up to 1.85; \autoref{tab:prompt_sens}) implies that faithfulness scores are substantially determined by evaluation methodology, not just model properties. We recommend future faithfulness evaluations include: (1)~prompt sensitivity ablations, (2)~no-CoT control conditions, (3)~stratification by baseline correctness, and (4)~per-benchmark reporting, since aggregate scores can mask benchmark-specific patterns.

\paragraph{Competent faithfulness vs.\ unfaithful bypassing.} The conditional faithfulness asymmetry (\autoref{tab:conditional}) helps distinguish these alternatives. If models were primarily \emph{reconstructing} removed steps from context (competent faithfulness), we would expect similar knockout rates regardless of baseline correctness. Instead, faithfulness is systematically \emph{lower} on correctly-solved items (6--28\%) than on incorrectly-solved items (18--54\%), with the gap ranging from $-$11.6pp to $-$29.4pp across models. This suggests that for correctly-solved items, models largely bypass the provided CoT to retrieve memorized or prior-derived answers.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Model scope}: Five models at Q4\_K\_M quantization ($\leq$9B), with only two medium-tier models. Additional medium-tier models (Mistral-7B, Gemma-2-9B) were dropped due to high error rates.
    \item \textbf{Knockout validity}: Conflates reconstruction with unfaithfulness; the conditional faithfulness analysis (\S\ref{sec:stratified}) partially mitigates but cannot fully resolve this without human annotation of model outputs.
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual prompt bias}: The consistency-checking instruction inflates scores (\S\ref{sec:controls}); the unbiased ablation quantifies but does not eliminate this confound.
    \item \textbf{Paraphrase metric}: Jaccard keyword similarity is a coarse structural proxy. The model generates its own paraphrases, risking circularity.
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility}: $T{=}0.0$ with seed 42 throughout; multi-seed robustness was infeasible. GPU non-determinism may cause minor variation.
    \item \textbf{Domain}: Mathematical and logical reasoning only; findings may not transfer to commonsense or factual reasoning.
    \item \textbf{Error exclusion bias}: JSON parse failures range from 0.5\% to 42.0\% (\autoref{tab:errors}). If error-prone problems systematically differ in faithfulness, exclusion could bias results.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

Demonstrating CoT unfaithfulness has trust implications for users relying on model explanations. Our findings are specific to the five models studied and should not be overgeneralized. All experiments use open-source models locally; no human subjects or personal data are involved.
